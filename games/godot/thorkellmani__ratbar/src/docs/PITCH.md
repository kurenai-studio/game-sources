# Rat Bar — Design Document

## The Premise

The owner of a failing late-night restaurant has lost his entire staff. They didn't quit over pay or hours — they quit because of the rats. The owner knew about them. He tolerated them, maybe ignored them. One day the staff had enough.

Now he's alone. The debt is real. The dinner service is tonight.

He walks through the kitchen and finds the rats mid-game-night. Cards, cigarettes, someone's made food. They don't scatter. They look at him. He looks at them.

He sees an opportunity. He bribes them — their vices, their food, their safety — and they go along with it, more or less. Not because they agreed to anything. They were just having a good time and this seemed fine.

That's the deal. That's the whole deal.

---

## The Allegory

This is a company town. The owner controls housing, food, and security. He holds existential leverage over the rats. The rats know it too, roughly.

What neither party fully grasps: the owner depends on the rats far more than they depend on him. Without them there is no bar, no income, no survival. The rats could find another basement. The owner has nothing.

The real antagonist is never the rats. It's the debt, the rent, the pressure to expand and turn a profit, the system that rewarded the owner for taking the opportunity when he saw it. He's not a monster. He's just doing what the logic of his situation demands. The game lets you feel the pull of that before showing you the cost.

---

## The Two Tones

The owner operates in the register of a professional kitchen — the pretense of standards, the health inspector, the reviews, the spreadsheet. Formal structure imposed on a chaotic situation. Slightly absurd.

The rats operate in a completely different register. Late night, low light, someone's always got a cigarette going. Not performing anything. Not making a statement. Just living as they see it, bad days and good days, shallow friendships and occasional genuine ones. Judgment free in the specific sense that nobody there is judging anyone else — not because they're virtuous, but because they don't care enough to.

The comedy and the tension both live in the collision between these two worlds.

---

## The Goals

**What the game presents:** Run a successful restaurant. Maximize profit. Keep the doors open.

**What actually happens:** Exploitation is unstable. Push too hard and rats burn out, get sick, die, or organize. The debt pressure never stops. There is no stable equilibrium on the exploitation path.

**The true goal:** Become a rat man. Stop managing and start belonging. Let the bar become what it actually is rather than what you need it to be. This requires the owner to voluntarily give up his leverage — which the game never forces him to do.

**The survival goal:** You can also just try to keep the restaurant alive as long as possible. That's a valid run. It will probably end badly.

---

## Core Loop

The owner never cooks. He manages conditions.

His tools are indirect: job assignment, the physical environment, what vices and food he makes available, who works near who. He cannot speak to the rats. He finds them disgusting. Everything is mediated.

The dinner service is the resolution moment — where his decisions from the day play out. How it goes depends on the rats' moods, relationships, and the social dynamics he may or may not have understood.

Between services he observes, adjusts, and makes decisions about resources. The rats do what rats do.

---

## The Rats

Every rat, regardless of personality, gravitates toward not working. Socializing is the default state. Work is always the exception, always requires conditions to be right.

The thing that makes rats functional — their social energy, their relationships, their vibe — is the same thing that makes them terrible employees. You cannot have one without the other. Suppress the chaos completely and you don't get productivity, you get burnout and unrest.

### What the Owner Sees

The owner's view of each rat is crude and practical. A manager's assessment:

- **Work Ethic** — how much they appear to be working
- **Appetite** — vice consumption, direct cost
- **Mood** — rough emotional read
- **Reliability** — do they show up, do they stay at post

These stats are the owner's projection. They are real but partial. A rat with high socialness and genuine ambition reads as low work ethic because he keeps stopping to talk. What's actually happening is invisible to the owner unless he pays close attention.

### What's Actually Happening

Beneath the owner's view, each rat has a real personality — a set of internal stats that drive their actual behavior. These are never displayed directly. The player builds understanding through observation over time.

**Personality stats (internal):**
- Chillness
- Riskiness
- Ambition
- Angriness
- Socialness
- Meanness

**Mood stats (internal, fluctuating):**
- Tiredness
- Boredom
- Stress
- Burnout level
- Hunger

These stats generate behavior and produce an archetype label — a classification used internally, never shown to the player. Two rats with the same archetype can behave differently depending on their specific stat spread and current mood.

### Archetypes

Archetypes are stat clusters, not fixed behavioral scripts. Most rats are common. Rarer archetypes emerge from unusual stat combinations and are harder to read.

The Organizer is rare. Genuine political consciousness combined with the social capital to actually move people is an uncommon combination. When morale is low and an Organizer is in the building, something is building quietly. The owner will probably not notice until it's too late.

### Rat-to-Rat Dynamics

Rats affect each other. Mood is contagious. Relationships develop through proximity and shared experience. A rat having a bad shift near a rat having a good one creates friction. A socially magnetic rat pulls everyone into conversation whether they want to be or not.

Most relationships are shallow. Some are warm. A few are genuinely antagonistic. The rats coexist without much reflection on it. The owner is largely blind to this unless he pays attention.

---

## Vices

Rats have vices: smoking, drinking, drugs, sex, chilling. Providing or withholding them is the owner's primary lever over mood and compliance.

Vices cost money, spread socially, and escalate. A rat whose needs are never met becomes a problem. A rat whose needs are always met drags others in.

---

## Jobs

Cook, server, dishwasher, grocer. Each has a location. Rats assigned to jobs navigate there and work — until they don't.

Job satisfaction depends on personality fit. A high-socialness rat stuck on dishes alone will drift. A low-ambition rat given too much responsibility will check out. The owner assigns jobs but cannot keep rats in them by force.

---

## The Rat Man Ending

The owner stops using leverage. He stops optimizing. He lets the bar become what it is. The rats don't suddenly become productive — that's not the point. The point is that the relationship becomes honest. The bar might make less money. It might close eventually. But for a while it's actually something.

This ending is never instructed or telegraphed. It emerges from a pattern of choices.

---

## Scope

The simulation should be legible but not transparent. Enough moving parts to generate surprising behavior, few enough that the player can build a real mental model over time.

- Five to six rat archetypes
- Four owner-facing stats per rat
- Six internal personality stats
- Five mood stats
- Five vices
- Four to five job types

Personalities are communicated through behavior, not UI. Art carries tone. The owner's spreadsheet brain is the UI. The gap between them is the game.
